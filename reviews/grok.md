**Shanna Maltby**  
Thank you for raising this. Technology deserves more attention in this election because it touches jobs, local businesses, municipal services and how we spend taxpayers’ money.  
As a business owner and founder of Earth Credit Canada, technology is part of my work. I’m comfortable using digital tools and exploring new applications, while recognizing that AI, quantum computing, robotics and 3D printing each require different expertise. A councillor must be willing to learn, ask informed questions and seek qualified advice.  
I see AI as a tool in a toolbox, something to help us, not something to become completely reliant on. When we ask AI to write a script, it needs prompts, inputs and direction. We still have a responsibility to check its work, apply our judgment and stand behind the results.  
As your Ward 1 councillor, I would advocate for a practical technology plan with regular reviews. We should identify where technology could improve services, test it through small projects and measure the results before committing significant money. Faster applications, easier access to information and less repetitive administration are areas worth exploring. Privacy, cybersecurity, human oversight and access for residents who aren’t comfortable online must be part of that work.  
I also want us to work with businesses, schools and training providers to help residents develop new skills and attract employers to Acton. Our youth should have opportunities to learn about these technologies and build careers here.  
Your funding question is a serious one. If people lose employment or income, their ability to pay taxes suffers, while demand for public support may grow. We cannot assume that taxpayers can simply absorb increasing costs.  
My approach would be to scrutinize spending, use technology where it delivers measurable savings, support a diverse local economy and plan for different financial scenarios. Savings should help protect essential services and keep taxes manageable. Where responsibilities extend beyond the Town, we need to advocate with regional, provincial and federal partners.  
Technology is moving forward, and we cannot afford to be left behind. We need to prepare thoughtfully, bring our community along and make sure progress benefits the people who live here.

**Mike Albano**  
Thank you for the questions, Chris.  
I have been using technology in one form or another throughout my working career and during my time on Council. In fact, I’m using technology right now to respond to this post.  
We can’t avoid technology it is everywhere, and it is changing by the minute. For me, being familiar with technology means continuing to learn.  
AI and other technologies are going to change the way we work. I believe that we need to adopt the changes while using them responsibly and where they provide real value. We should invest in technology when it can reduce administrative costs, improve productivity and improve services.  
We have seen this before. During the Industrial Revolution, there were many concerns that new technologies would replace workers and eliminate jobs. While technology did change the workforce, it also created new opportunities and changed the way we lived and worked.  
In my opinion, we need to keep up with changes and don’t fear them.

**Jason Brass**  
Chris, thanks for raising this.  
As Mayor, I would focus on finding efficiencies that may not be visible today. Could better technology identify duplicate work, reduce paperwork, uncover purchasing savings, improve scheduling or flag maintenance issues before they become expensive repairs? Those are opportunities worth exploring with the staff who understand our services best.  
The goal is to give our employees better tools and more time to serve residents. AI, robotics, 3D printing and quantum computing have different potential uses. **Alex Hilson**  
Thanks for the question, Chris, and apologies for the slow response. The speed of technology is continuing to increase and I think it’s tough for many sectors, including government, to keep track. I do use technology and am as familiar with it as I think most adults are in this day and age.  
At the Ontario Municipal Conference a year or two back, I attended a session on using AI and computer modelling to better plan municipal budgeting, repairs and forecasting. The Province put forth legislation a couple years ago about Asset Management which is making municipalities categorize and record all their infrastructure so we can better plan and budget going forward. Halton Hills has gone through this process and I support it. The Town has also continued to roll out Copilot licenses to help make staff more efficient. Meanwhile, our library (where I have served on their board) is using 3D printing to print medals for Battle of the Books and for other activities, saving us time and making us more efficient. At the Regional level, the Region is starting to use technology to make smarter intersections where traffic lights better respond to actual traffic volumes at different times of day rather than using the same time intervals whether there is traffic or not. There are, of course, all sorts of examples and the possibilities are nearly endless. We will need to continue to monitor what those possibilities are and I support being creative with what those possibilities might be.  
To your question about funding the public sector as the private sector sheds jobs, I think we are really only at the start of the next technological revolution. In my mind, it’s very tough to imagine how different our working lives and different sectors might look in the next couple decades as automation continues to a whole new level. We need to use the tools available to us and benefit from those efficiencies. People will still need jobs or ways of feeding their families, though, and I think we will navigate those changes with the rest of society as things continue to change (as we have in the past with other major technological revolutions throughout history).

**Hillary Spurr**  
Hi Chris,  
There’s no doubt that AI is advancing at record speed, and we need to stay informed and adaptable as these technologies evolve.  
When it comes to the public and private sectors, I think we’ve already seen a major shift from when our generation entered post-secondary education to where we are today. We were often encouraged to believe university was the primary path to success, whereas today, we’re doing a much better job of showing young people the opportunities and earning potential that come with skilled trades. I see this first hand every day. We still have a shortage of skilled trade workers but I’m hopeful the numbers will continue to climb.  
COVID also gave us a clear picture of how important essential workers are to keeping our communities running. As society changes, our priorities need to evolve with it. We must continue investing in firefighters, police officers, paramedics, healthcare workers and skilled tradespeople whose hands-on expertise and human judgment are difficult to replace with AI.  
The next generation entering the workforce will have grown up with AI much like our generation grew up with social media. We need to prepare them to use these tools effectively while ensuring we continue to value the people, skills and essential services our communities depend on and we as leaders need to be prepared as well.

**Anthony Galati**  
I’m not a user of AI, perhaps to my detriment as I know that it can be a useful tool if applied correctly. I know that it can help us be more efficient and cut down on a lot of tasks like proofreading, editing and sourcing of references. However, it shouldn’t be used exclusively for writing reports or other relevant documents required in the workplace.  
As far as employers laying off workers, I believe that instead they could cut their hours back substantially while still paying them their regular wages. The super efficiency of AI would still allow them to turn a profit while giving their staff a much needed break. I reference a time when Mr Ford from the Ford Motor Company started using automation to build his vehicles, relying less on hands on performance from his staff. Well what did he do? Instead of laying off workers, he gave them Saturdays off! At the time, most people were working 6 day weeks. He was one of the first to go to a 5 day work week. Maybe we need to take that a step further and start giving workers a 4 day work week since the use of AI is improving efficiency by a large margin. It’s been talked about before and I believe it’s a relevant conversation to have again.
